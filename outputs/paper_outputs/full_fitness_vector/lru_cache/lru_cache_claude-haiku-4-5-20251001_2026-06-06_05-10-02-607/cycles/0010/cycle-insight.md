# Cycle 0010 Insight

## Cohort outcome

Three orthogonal optimizations targeting frequency-tracking bottlenecks were tested: cache-locality-optimized bucket structure with inline node pointers (V001), reduced-precision quantized frequency levels (V002), and opportunistic batch buffering of promotion updates (V003). No variant achieved a net win. Each succeeded on narrow tail-latency metrics (V001: put p99 -3%, V002: get p99 -39% and put p99 -26%, V003: put p99 -14%) but regressed median latencies (all three: get/put p50 +10-35%) and introduced subtle correctness or selectivity trade-offs that negate the improvements. The cohort demonstrates that the Cycle 0007 baseline (batched queue-flush with saturation-aware bucketing) is locally optimal, and that further single-axis improvements require accepting unfavorable Pareto trade-offs.

## Patterns

- **Optimization complexity on the critical path trades median latency for tail-latency gains at an unfavorable ratio**: V001's cache-locality packing reduced pointer-chasing in high-saturation bucket scans (putting p99 -3%) but added conditional logic in bucket header management that regressed get p50 +35% on the common simple-mode path. V002's quantized promotion thresholds reduced bucket relocations (get p99 -39%, put p99 -26%) but introduced branch complexity in the `should_promote()` check, regressing both get and put p50 (+31% and +15%). V003's vector append for batch buffering improved put p99 -14% but the buffer-management overhead on every get became the new bottleneck, causing get p50 +28% and get p99 +27% regression. This consistent pattern reveals that hot-path complexity for tail-latency improvements encounters diminishing returns on this light-load, underutilized workload where median latency (not tail) dominates the cost profile.

- **Batching infrastructure must be nearly invisible on the hot path, or it becomes the new bottleneck**: V003 applied Cycle 0007's successful batching insight (queue-flush coalescing) to frequency bucket updates, using deterministic threshold-based draining (buffer half-full at 8 entries) rather than per-operation conditional checks. However, the vector append operation itself (append + branched drain check) proved more expensive than the deferred bucket updates would be, causing get p50 +28% and get p99 +27%. This teaches that while algorithmic batching (deterministic, not conditional) reduced overhead in Cycle 0007's context, the batching primitive itself must have negligible cost; heavy-weight buffering (Vec allocations, conditional checks) trades one overhead for another.

- **Hit rate is now an algorithmic invariant; structure and precision changes no longer yield selectivity gains**: V001 degraded hit rate -2.3% despite packing node pointers more efficiently. V002 degraded hit rate -1.85% despite testing whether coarse quantization (4 levels vs. unbounded) captured the same selectivity. V003 maintained hit rate at baseline. Across Cycles 0003–0010, hit rate has remained pinned at ~0.66-0.69 regardless of frequency representation (unbounded, quantized, time-windowed, pure LRU). This confirms that window size, not precision or structure, determines selectivity on this workload. Eviction-algorithm changes have exhausted their returns on hit-rate optimization.

- **The optimization landscape has reached local equilibrium; single-axis improvements conflict with multi-metric trade-offs**: All three variants targeted complementary bottlenecks (pointer-chase cost, frequency precision overhead, bucket-update latency) identified in prior-cycle analysis. Yet none improved overall, and each regressed at least one critical metric (hit rate or median latency). The Cycle 0007 baseline's design—batched queue-flush for deterministic amortization, saturation-aware bucketing, bounded-window eviction—appears to be a tightly tuned local optimum where improving one metric requires accepting proportional or greater regression elsewhere. The cohort provides no evidence of further single-axis optimization leverage.

- **Underutilized workloads expose that optimizations targeting rare code paths have negative expected value**: V001's cache-locality optimization targets the bounded-window bucket scan during high-saturation evictions. Yet Cycle 0009 confirmed this workload operates in simple mode (unbounded eviction, no bucketing) throughout the trace. The optimization is invisible, while the conditional overhead in common-case bucket header management is always paid. V003's saturation-aware batching similarly assumes frequent promotion updates; on light load, the buffer accumulates slowly and drains rarely, making the per-operation append cost dominant. This pattern—optimizing for rare paths at cost to common paths—is masked in synthetic or write-heavy workloads where saturation is high; it emerges clearly here because saturation never materialized.

## Open questions

- Can the baseline's get p50 regression from Cycle 0007 (20%) be recovered without sacrificing the p99 improvements? All three variants here failed to improve median latency, suggesting the regression may be an inherent trade-off of batched queue-flush. Is there a batch-size, drain-frequency, or threshold tuning that recovers median latency?

- Is this workload's sustained light utilization the constraint limiting optimization? Would any of these three variants show positive ROI on higher-saturation traces, write-heavy synthetic workloads, or longer-running episodes where saturation dynamics change? Empirical saturation profiling of the test trace might reveal whether optimization landscape shifts at higher load.

- Does this cohort's failure on single-axis improvements mean orthogonal axes (memory hierarchy, contention reduction, parallelism) are the remaining frontier, or is the baseline already near-optimal for this workload class?

## Continuation

Cycle 0010 provides strong evidence that the Cycle 0007 baseline is locally optimal and that further algorithmic optimization on the frequency-tracking axis has exhausted returns. All three variants attempted complementary improvements (structure cache locality, precision reduction, deferred batch buffering) yet each introduced hot-path complexity that regressed median latency more than tail improvements could offset. The common lesson across all cycles: frequency-tracking infrastructure is load-bearing for both O(1) eviction (Cycle 0009 V002) and hit-rate saturation (Cycles 0003-0010), and any optimization that adds cost to the critical path (hot-mode get/put) must provide proportional benefit. On this light-load, underutilized workload, that threshold is not met by any single-axis strategy.

Next cycle should: (a) validate that the Cycle 0007 baseline is robust across diverse workloads (higher saturation levels, write-heavy synthetic traces, longer episodes) rather than attempting further algorithmic optimization; (b) if single-axis improvements are still desired, focus on making existing infrastructure cheaper (can bucket updates be lock-free? vectorized? branch-predicted better?) rather than replacing it; (c) pivot to orthogonal optimization axes entirely—memory hierarchy (cache-line alignment, prefetch-friendly traversal), contention reduction via lock-free updates, or parallelism via sharded structures—since the frequency-tracking and eviction-strategy axes appear exhausted; or (d) empirically profile the test trace's saturation distribution to establish whether high-saturation regimes ever emerge, and if so, whether optimizations designed for high load show different trade-offs in those regimes.
